# Dietary, Nutritional, and Lifestyle Interventions in Infertility Care

### Current Evidence from Human Intervention Studies in Female and Male Infertility

**Updated September 2026**


Hidefumi Kuwata

---

## Executive Summary

This report reviews current evidence on dietary, nutritional, supplemental, and lifestyle interventions used in infertility care, with particular emphasis on randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and other human intervention studies.

A central theme across both female and male infertility is the distinction between **intermediate biological outcomes** and **clinically meaningful reproductive outcomes**. Improvements in ovarian response, oocyte yield, embryo-related measures, semen parameters, oxidative stress, or sperm DNA fragmentation do not necessarily translate into higher pregnancy or live-birth rates.

Several interventions produce measurable biological effects, but the strength of evidence varies considerably according to the population studied, the intervention, the outcome assessed, and the quality of the available trials.

### Key findings

**1. Female infertility: a shift in the interpretation of evidence**

Among women with diminished ovarian reserve (DOR) or poor ovarian response (POR), **coenzyme Q10 (CoQ10)** currently shows one of the more promising signals among nutritional interventions, particularly for ovarian response, oocyte yield, and embryo-related outcomes. However, evidence that CoQ10 increases live birth remains insufficient.

By contrast, **dehydroepiandrosterone (DHEA)** illustrates how clinical interpretation can change as higher-quality evidence accumulates. Earlier small studies and non-randomized reports suggested potential benefit in women with DOR/POR. More recent evidence, including a large RCT, the 2024 Cochrane review, and the 2025 ESHRE guideline, does not support a meaningful improvement in live birth and does not support routine use in low responders.

**2. Male infertility: improvement in sperm biology does not necessarily translate into live birth**

In men, antioxidants and metabolically active nutrients such as **CoQ10, L-carnitine, N-acetylcysteine (NAC), and selenium** may improve semen parameters and, in selected studies, sperm DNA fragmentation.

However, improvements in semen quality or sperm DNA fragmentation have not been shown consistently to increase live birth. This gap between biological improvement and patient-important reproductive outcomes is one of the most important limitations of the male infertility supplementation literature.

Large randomized trials of broad antioxidant combinations and folic acid plus zinc have failed to demonstrate meaningful improvements in live birth.

**3. Sperm DNA fragmentation should be treated as an intermediate outcome**

Sperm DNA fragmentation (SDF), often quantified using the DNA fragmentation index (DFI), has become an important biological endpoint in male infertility research. Antioxidant interventions may reduce SDF in some studies, but results are inconsistent.

More importantly, a reduction in SDF should not be assumed to result in an increased probability of live birth. At present, SDF is best regarded as a biologically relevant intermediate outcome rather than an established surrogate for live birth.

**4. Strongest current evidence and greatest research opportunity are not the same**

Interventions with the most mature clinical evidence are not necessarily those with the greatest research novelty.

CoQ10 represents an intervention with a relatively developed human evidence base. In contrast, the **arginine–ornithine–polyamine pathway**, including putrescine, spermidine, and spermine, is biologically compelling but remains poorly studied in modern human infertility intervention trials.

This mismatch between mechanistic plausibility and clinical evidence represents an important opportunity for future translational research.

# 1. Scope and Approach

This report summarizes intervention studies investigating dietary patterns, nutrients, dietary supplements, exercise, weight reduction, and related interventions in infertility care.

The analysis focuses primarily on human intervention studies and, where available, randomized controlled trials and systematic reviews of RCTs.

The following dimensions are considered separately:

1. **Population studied**
2. **Intervention**
3. **Biological or reproductive outcomes affected**
4. **Strength of the human evidence**
5. **Evidence for pregnancy and live birth**
6. **Current clinical interpretation**

DHEA is included despite not being a nutrient in the strict sense. It is an adrenal steroid precursor that has been widely studied as an oral adjunct in women with DOR or POR and therefore provides an informative comparison with nutritional interventions.

A major principle throughout this review is that **evidence strength and evidence of efficacy are not the same thing**.

A large, well-conducted RCT showing no benefit provides stronger evidence than a small uncontrolled study showing a large apparent benefit.

---

# 2. Evidence Categories Used in This Report

The following categories are used to facilitate comparison among interventions.

These categories are **not formal GRADE ratings** and should not be interpreted as such.

| Evidence category                        | Definition used in this report                                                                                                                             |
| ---------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **A — Relatively strong human evidence** | Multiple RCTs, a large RCT, or RCT-based meta-analyses are available, allowing the overall direction of evidence to be assessed with reasonable confidence |
| **B — Moderate human evidence**          | Multiple intervention studies or RCTs exist, but sample size, heterogeneity, bias, inconsistent outcomes, or other limitations remain important            |
| **C — Limited human evidence**           | Evidence is based mainly on one or a few small RCTs, older trials, or markedly conflicting findings                                                        |
| **D — Exploratory human evidence**       | Non-randomized studies, case series, or small exploratory human studies predominate                                                                        |
| **E — Preclinical evidence**             | Evidence is derived mainly from animal or cellular studies, with no established infertility intervention evidence in humans                                |

Importantly, an intervention may receive an **A-level evidence category because there is strong evidence that it does not improve live birth**.

# 4. DHEA in DOR/POR: How Higher-Quality Evidence Changed Clinical Interpretation

DHEA is an instructive example of how the apparent efficacy of an intervention can change as the quality of the evidence improves.

Early studies of DHEA in women with diminished ovarian reserve or poor ovarian response reported potentially favorable effects on:

* antral follicle count (AFC),
* anti-Müllerian hormone (AMH),
* oocyte yield,
* number of metaphase II oocytes,
* embryo quality,
* and clinical pregnancy.

These findings contributed to widespread clinical interest in DHEA pretreatment.

Earlier meta-analyses also reported apparently favorable effects on clinical pregnancy and, in some analyses, live birth. However, the magnitude of benefit was consistently reduced when analyses were restricted to randomized trials.

A major change in interpretation came from a relatively large randomized controlled trial involving **821 women with poor ovarian response defined according to the Bologna criteria**.

Live-birth rates were:

**8.8% in the DHEA group vs. 9.0% in the placebo group.**

There were also no meaningful differences in:

* number of oocytes retrieved,
* clinical pregnancy,
* miscarriage,
* or cumulative live birth.

The 2024 Cochrane review further reported:

**DHEA vs. placebo/no treatment for live birth or ongoing pregnancy: OR 1.30, 95% CI 0.95–1.76; 9 RCTs; 1,433 women**

and concluded, with moderate-certainty evidence, that DHEA probably results in **little to no difference**.

The 2025 ESHRE ovarian stimulation guideline similarly reviewed trials in which DHEA was generally administered at approximately 75 mg/day for 6–12 weeks and issued a strong recommendation against its routine use in low responders.

The most appropriate current interpretation is therefore:

> **DHEA was widely adopted on the basis of encouraging early studies, but the accumulation of higher-quality randomized evidence has not supported a meaningful improvement in live birth.**

An additional methodological issue is that **DOR and POR are not identical clinical entities**.

DOR refers to diminished ovarian reserve, generally characterized using biomarkers such as AMH and AFC, whereas POR refers to an inadequate response to ovarian stimulation.

The DHEA literature includes both types of populations, and this heterogeneity may partly explain differences among earlier studies.

# 6. Sperm DNA Fragmentation in Male Infertility

When evaluating nutritional and antioxidant interventions in male infertility, conventional semen measures such as sperm concentration, motility, and morphology provide only part of the biological picture.

Sperm DNA fragmentation has therefore become an increasingly important research endpoint.

Oxidative stress may contribute to sperm DNA damage through pathways such as:

**increased reactive oxygen species (ROS)**
→ **lipid peroxidation**
→ **mitochondrial dysfunction**
→ **sperm DNA damage**
→ **increased SDF / DFI**

This biological model provides a strong rationale for studying antioxidants such as:

* CoQ10,
* L-carnitine,
* NAC,
* selenium,
* vitamin C,
* and vitamin E.

The key question is whether antioxidant treatment can reduce SDF or DFI in addition to improving conventional semen parameters.

---

## 6.1 Some Intervention Trials Do Show Improvement in SDF/DFI

Positive randomized studies do exist.

Recent antioxidant trials have reported reductions in DFI, and some placebo-controlled studies using combinations such as CoQ10 plus L-carnitine have also reported improvements in sperm DNA integrity.

Whole-food intervention data are also noteworthy.

In the **FERTINUTS** randomized trial, consumption of **60 g/day of mixed nuts for 14 weeks** significantly reduced sperm DNA fragmentation while also improving several conventional semen measures.

These findings support the conclusion that:

> **Dietary or antioxidant interventions can reduce sperm DNA fragmentation in at least some male populations.**

However, this statement requires important qualification.

---

## 6.2 SDF/DFI Improvement Is Not Consistent Across Trials

A 2022 systematic review evaluating antioxidant supplementation and sperm DNA fragmentation found:

* significant improvement in DFI in **4 studies**,
* and no significant improvement in **5 studies**.

Thus, the available intervention literature was almost evenly divided.

In a double-blind placebo-controlled randomized trial of **77 men with elevated DFI**, six months of antioxidant therapy did not significantly improve DFI compared with placebo.

The **MOXI trial** similarly found no meaningful improvement.

DFI was:

**29.5% vs. 28.0%**

and cumulative live birth was:

**15% vs. 24%**

with no evidence that antioxidant therapy improved the final reproductive outcome.

More recently, the **SUMMER randomized clinical trial**, involving more than 1,000 men seeking fertility care, found no improvement in ongoing pregnancy with a multinutrient antioxidant formulation and no meaningful difference in important secondary outcomes, including DFI.

The appropriate conclusion is therefore not that antioxidants consistently reduce DFI, but rather that:

> **Some interventions reduce sperm DNA fragmentation in selected studies, whereas other well-conducted randomized trials show no effect.**

---

# 7. Improvements in Sperm DNA Fragmentation Are Not Equivalent to Improvements in Live Birth

This distinction is one of the most important issues in the interpretation of male infertility nutrition studies.

Reproductive intervention studies can be viewed as a hierarchy of endpoints:

**Reduced oxidative stress**
↓
**Reduced sperm DNA fragmentation**
↓
**Improved sperm count, motility, or morphology**
↓
**Improved fertilization and embryo development**
↓
**Clinical pregnancy**
↓
**Live birth**

Improvement at one level does not guarantee improvement at the next.

For example, an antioxidant may:

* reduce a marker of oxidative stress,
* improve progressive sperm motility,
* and possibly reduce DFI,

without having a measurable effect on the probability of a live birth.

A 2023 meta-analysis including **45 RCTs and 4,332 men** reported improvements in:

* sperm concentration,
* progressive motility,
* total motility,
* normal morphology,
* and spontaneous pregnancy.

However, the analysis did **not establish a significant improvement in live birth**, and the small number of RCTs that reported sperm DNA fragmentation did not demonstrate a clear pooled benefit.

Likewise, a 2025 meta-analysis of **50 randomized trials** concluded that there was no convincing evidence that dietary supplementation in male infertility improves either pregnancy or live birth. Overall certainty of evidence was generally low or very low.

The most appropriate overall interpretation is therefore:

> **Some antioxidant interventions may improve semen parameters or sperm DNA fragmentation. However, improvements in SDF/DFI are not consistent across RCTs, and even when SDF/DFI improves, it has not been established that this results in a higher live-birth rate.**

This distinction is clinically important.

**SDF is a biologically meaningful intermediate endpoint, but it has not been established as a validated surrogate endpoint for live birth.**

The AUA/ASRM guideline similarly recognizes associations between elevated sperm DNA fragmentation and adverse reproductive outcomes but does not recommend routine SDF testing as part of the initial evaluation of infertile men, in part because of heterogeneity in assays, cutoffs, and study design.

# 8. Cross-Sex Interpretation of the Evidence

## 8.1 Female Infertility

The usefulness of nutritional or adjunctive interventions in women depends strongly on the underlying infertility phenotype.

### DOR and POR

For women with DOR or POR, **CoQ10** currently represents one of the more promising nutritional interventions.

A meta-analysis of six RCTs found relatively consistent signals for:

* increased oocyte yield,
* increased numbers of good-quality embryos,
* and improved clinical pregnancy.

However, a definitive increase in live birth has not yet been established.

The situation with **DHEA** is almost the reverse.

DHEA has been studied extensively and used clinically for many years. Yet when higher-quality randomized evidence is prioritized, the apparent benefit becomes much less convincing.

Thus:

> **DHEA has a relatively mature evidence base, but that evidence is now largely negative for improvement in live birth.**

This is an important example of how encouraging early studies can be overturned or substantially modified by larger randomized trials.

### PCOS

Vitamin D, myo-inositol, and NAC all show biologically plausible and, in some cases, clinically interesting signals in women with PCOS.

Reported effects include:

* improved ovulation,
* changes in endometrial thickness,
* improved oocyte maturation,
* and higher clinical pregnancy in selected trials.

However, no supplement can currently be said with high certainty to improve live birth across women with PCOS or female infertility more broadly.

Correcting an identified nutrient deficiency should also be distinguished from using the same nutrient pharmacologically in an individual who is already nutritionally sufficient.

### Obesity and Lifestyle Intervention

Dietary modification, physical activity, and weight reduction have important benefits for metabolic health and may increase the probability of spontaneous conception in some women with obesity.

However:

> **The proposition that ART should routinely be delayed in order to achieve weight loss because doing so will increase live birth is not supported by large randomized trials.**

This does not diminish the broader health value of weight management; it simply illustrates the need to distinguish general health benefit from demonstrated improvement in fertility-treatment outcomes.

---

## 8.2 Male Infertility

The gap between **intermediate outcomes** and **final reproductive outcomes** is even more pronounced in male infertility.

CoQ10, L-carnitine, NAC, and selenium-containing interventions may improve one or more of:

* sperm concentration,
* motility,
* morphology,
* oxidative-stress markers,
* and, in selected studies, sperm DNA fragmentation.

CoQ10 and carnitine in particular have shown relatively reproducible effects on conventional semen parameters.

However:

> **A sperm sample that appears biologically “better” does not necessarily mean that the probability of a live birth has increased.**

The major systematic reviews and large randomized trials published to date have not established a convincing live-birth advantage for antioxidant supplementation as a general strategy in male infertility.

The same caution applies to sperm DNA fragmentation.

The biological pathway:

**antioxidant intervention**
→ **lower oxidative stress**
→ **lower SDF/DFI**
→ **improved sperm function**

is mechanistically plausible and is supported by some intervention trials.

But:

**lower SDF/DFI**
≠
**proven increase in live birth**

This distinction should be made explicit whenever antioxidant trials are interpreted or communicated.

# 10. Established Evidence vs. Emerging Research Opportunities

The interventions with the strongest current clinical evidence are not necessarily the interventions with the greatest scientific novelty.

This distinction is especially relevant when considering future research.

## 10.1 A relatively mature field: CoQ10

CoQ10 has now been studied in multiple human infertility trials.

In women with DOR/POR, it has produced relatively consistent signals involving ovarian response and embryo-related outcomes.

In men, it has produced relatively consistent improvements in selected semen parameters.

Thus, future CoQ10 research is less about determining whether the compound has any biological effect and more about determining:

* which patients benefit most,
* the optimal dose,
* the optimal duration,
* whether pretreatment should be phenotype-specific,
* and, most importantly, whether biological improvements ultimately increase live birth.

## 10.2 A mature but increasingly negative field: DHEA

DHEA demonstrates a different research trajectory.

Early biological plausibility and small clinical studies led to substantial enthusiasm.

However, larger randomized evidence did not confirm an improvement in live birth.

For future studies, the challenge is therefore not simply to repeat DHEA trials in broadly defined low responders, but to determine whether any biologically defined subgroup exists in which androgen supplementation might still have a clinically meaningful effect.

## 10.3 A major translational gap: the arginine–ornithine–polyamine axis

By comparison, polyamine metabolism is biologically important but clinically underexplored.

A simplified pathway is:

**L-Arginine**
↓
**L-Ornithine**
↓
**Putrescine**
↓
**Spermidine**
↓
**Spermine**

Arginine can also enter the nitric oxide pathway:

**L-Arginine**
↓ nitric oxide synthase
**Nitric oxide (NO)**

This creates two potentially important reproductive pathways:

1. **Arginine → NO**, with potential effects on vascular function and reproductive tissue perfusion
2. **Arginine → ornithine → polyamines**, with potential effects on cellular growth, mitochondrial function, chromatin biology, oocyte quality, sperm function, and embryo development

Preclinical work on spermidine in ovarian aging is particularly notable.

Animal studies suggest that spermidine supplementation can improve:

* mitophagy,
* mitochondrial quality control,
* oocyte competence,
* ovarian function,
* and reproductive capacity.

In men, spermine and spermidine are abundant components of seminal plasma and have long been linked biologically to sperm function.

Nevertheless, modern randomized infertility trials directly targeting polyamine metabolism are remarkably sparse.

This creates a clear discrepancy:

> **Strong mechanistic plausibility, but limited modern human intervention evidence.**

For this reason, the arginine–ornithine–polyamine pathway may represent an attractive target for translational reproductive-nutrition research.

Potential future human studies could examine:

* women with DOR or reproductive aging,
* men with elevated oxidative stress or abnormal SDF,
* circulating and follicular-fluid polyamine profiles,
* seminal-plasma polyamines,
* oocyte and embryo endpoints,
* sperm DNA integrity,
* clinical pregnancy,
* and ultimately live birth.

---

# 11. Conclusions

Current evidence shows that dietary, nutritional, supplemental, and lifestyle interventions can produce measurable biological effects in both female and male infertility.

However, the clinical meaning of these effects depends critically on the endpoint being studied.

In women with DOR/POR, **CoQ10** currently shows one of the more promising signals among nutritional interventions, particularly for ovarian response and embryo-related outcomes.

By contrast, **DHEA**, despite a long history of clinical interest and a relatively large research literature, is no longer supported as a routine intervention for improving live birth in low responders when higher-quality randomized evidence and current guidelines are taken into account.

In women with PCOS, vitamin D, myo-inositol, and NAC remain biologically and clinically interesting, but evidence for a reliable live-birth benefit remains insufficient.

In men, **CoQ10, carnitine, NAC, selenium-containing interventions, and some dietary approaches** may improve conventional semen parameters and, in selected studies, sperm DNA fragmentation.

The most important interpretative principle is:

> **Improvement in semen parameters or sperm DNA fragmentation is not equivalent to improvement in pregnancy or live birth.**

Antioxidant interventions may reduce SDF/DFI in some trials, but this effect is not consistent across studies. Furthermore, even when SDF/DFI improves, a causal increase in live birth has not been established.

Thus, intermediate biological endpoints should not be treated as validated substitutes for patient-important reproductive outcomes.

Finally, the intervention with the strongest existing clinical evidence is not necessarily the intervention with the greatest potential for future discovery.

CoQ10 represents a relatively mature clinical research area.

The **arginine–ornithine–polyamine pathway**, including putrescine, spermidine, and spermine, represents a contrasting example: reproductive biology provides a compelling mechanistic rationale, whereas modern human intervention evidence remains sparse.

Closing this gap between biological plausibility and rigorous human evidence—ideally using clinical pregnancy and live birth as key outcomes—should be an important objective of future reproductive-nutrition research.
